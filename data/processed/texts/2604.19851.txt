Approach to Condorcet’s Paradox

1Department of Computer Science, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, PA

Abstract
Computational social choice studies how conflicting individual preferences are aggregated into a col-
lective decision, with applications to elections, AI evaluation/alignment, and recommendation systems.
We focus on settings where n voters (e.g., human evaluators or algorithmic benchmarks) each rank m
alternatives (e.g., human candidates or AI models/outputs), and the goal is to select a committee of k
alternatives. One measure of the (in)stability of the outcome is the number of voters who prefer an
unchosen alternative to all alternatives in the committee. If this number is less than half the electorate,
the committee is a Condorcet winning set, which may not exist if k ≤2. More generally, there exist
instances where any committee of size k has at least a 2/(k+1) −ϵ fraction of the electorate preferring
another alternative, for arbitrarily small ϵ. While recent work has made remarkable progress narrowing
the gap, it is currently unknown whether this lower bound is tight.
We develop a mixed-integer linear programming framework that computes exact bounds for fixed
parameter values. Our approach depends only on m and k; by modeling electorates as distributions
over rankings, we eliminate any dependence on n. This provides a middle ground between theoretical
approaches that seek to give bounds depending solely on k, which are general but currently not tight, and
previous SAT-based approaches, which scale poorly with n and cannot establish generalizable bounds.
Our programs allow us to rule out counterexamples over arbitrary electorate sizes and obtain tight bounds
for fixed m and k. Further, our framework provides a pathway for proving general upper bounds. In
particular, we show a linear regime, k ≥8m/9, in which the conjectured 2/(k+1) upper bound indeed
holds, providing the first results that match known lower bounds. Beyond stability, our framework can
characterize the relationships among other social choice axioms, which we demonstrate by producing
minimal incomparability results.

Introduction
Computational social choice provides a formal framework for studying how individual preferences can be
aggregated into collective decisions. Preference aggregation underlies many high-stakes decision-making
processes in today’s world, including elections for representative governments [Arrow, 1951], AI evaluation
and alignment [Conitzer et al., 2024], recommendation systems [Pennock et al., 2000, Skowron et al., 2016],
and scarce resource allocation [Freedman et al., 2020, Zilberstein et al., 2026]. Across these settings, a
central challenge is to identify collective outcomes that accommodate conflicting preferences.
In this paper, we consider elections in which n voters each rank m alternatives. These terms encompass
more than political elections: voters may represent human evaluators or benchmark tasks, while alternatives
may represent candidates for office or employment, recommended items, or AI models and their outputs.
A classic obstacle in ranked choice voting is the Condorcet paradox [De Condorcet, 1785]: there are
elections in which selecting any single alternative as the winner results in a majority of voters preferring
an alternative. We illustrate such an election in Table 1. In the worst case over all elections, the fraction
of the electorate that prefers another alternative over the selected winner can come arbitrarily close to one,
rendering single-winner systems theoretically unstable.
Selecting a committee of k alternatives is a natural relaxation of the single-winner setting. Committee
selection also arises in many real-world domains (e.g., shortlisting multiple job applicants for interviewing
or electing parliamentary representatives). Here, a committee’s stability can be measured analogously to
a single winner through the largest fraction of voters who prefer any single unselected alternative to every
committee member. A committee is called a Condorcet winning set (CWS) if this fraction is less than one
half [Elkind et al., 2011, 2015]. This raises a natural question: how large must a committee be to guarantee
this form of stability, regardless of the number of alternatives, the number of voters, or their preferences?
This question was first posed in 2015 by Elkind et al. [2015], who established that a CWS of size
O(log m) always exists. On the negative side, they gave an election where no two-member committee is
a CWS. More generally, their construction can be extended to show that for every α < 2/(k+1), there are
elections in which every committee of size k faces an unselected alternative that at least an α fraction of
voters prefer to every committee member Charikar et al. [2025]. The gap remained open for almost a decade,
until Charikar et al. [2025] showed six alternatives always suffice for a CWS, independently of the number
of alternatives or voters, and Song et al. [2026] improved this bound to five. More generally, ideas from the
latter can be used in tandem with prior proof techniques Jiang et al. [2020], Charikar et al. [2025] to prove
the existence of a k-member committee where no other alternative is preferred by a ≈4.910/k fraction of the
voters Charikar et al. [2026]. Despite these advances, it remains one of the most elusive questions in social
choice as to whether three or four alternatives always suffice to form a CWS, and, more generally, whether
the lower bound of 2/(k+1) is in fact tight, as conjectured by Charikar et al. [2025]. Closing this gap requires
either stronger existence guarantees or elections that demonstrate their impossibility.
Our contributions
We reframe worst-case committee stability as an optimization problem over election
instances and develop a framework based on mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) that computes exact
worst-case values of α for specific parameters. By modeling electorates as distributions over rankings, our
formulation depends only on the number of alternatives m and committee size k, not on the number of
voters n. We develop two main programs with different advantages. The first program, MILP, computes the
exact worst-case value of α for fixed m and k. We develop a number of optimizations to scale MILP, such
as symmetry breaking, and we prove the correctness of these methods. We computationally establish that,
in every election with less than nine alternatives and a committee size greater than two, a CWS exists. More

of their nonexistence) via generative AI, which may at times lead to hard-to-verify proofs [de Boer, 2026,
Chang, 2026], our automated reasoning approach exhaustively searches over the space of constructions and
produces independently checkable certificates. These outputs are easily verifiable and can be immediately
translated into concise, human-readable proofs. Our minimum-size counterexamples of incomparability
from Section 6 illustrate this.

Related work
Committee selection and proportionality
Selecting a committee of candidates that proportionally rep-
resents the preferences of an electorate is a classic problem in social choice [Droop, 1881]. When voters
provide preferences via rankings of candidates, the concept of a Condorcet winning set (CWS) as a stable
set of winners was formalized by Elkind et al. [2011]. This led to the notion of the Condorcet dimension of
an election: the smallest k such that a size k CWS exists [Elkind et al., 2015]. The Condorcet dimension has
been extended to other settings beyond ranked choice voting, such as elections in which voter preferences
are measured by distances to candidates [Lassota et al., 2025].
The generalization of CWS to an arbitrary fraction of voters is closely linked to the literature on stable
committees Jiang et al. [2020], Cheng et al. [2019], where a committee is said to be core-stable (or in the
core) if no x% of the electorate can all be better off with another subcommittee that only uses x% of the seats.
In the ranking setting (where each voter’s utility is determined by their favorite candidate in the committee),
this is equivalent to requiring that there be no alternative that is preferred by at least 1/k fraction of the voters
(or strictly greater than 1/(k+1), using the more stringent Droop quota Brill et al. [2020]). While the existing
counterexamples showing the 2/(k+1) lower bound rule out the existence of core-stable committees in this
setting, upper bounds prove the existence of approximately stable committees.
Beyond committee stability, prior literature has also proposed other axioms capturing proportionality and
fair representation in multi-winner elections [Aziz et al., 2017]. Brill and Peters [2023] introduced Rank-
PJR+, Rank-EJR+, and rank-priceability, extending these proportionality notions to ranked preferences from
the approval-based setting; the latter builds on approval-based priceability [Peters and Skowron, 2020].
Automated reasoning and social choice
There is a growing body of work leveraging automated reasoning
to prove or disprove conjectures in social choice theory Geist and Peters [2017]. Early work successfully
applied automated reasoning to rederive impossibility theorems Tang and Lin [2008, 2009], such as that of
Arrow Arrow [1951] and Gibbard–Satterthwaite Gibbard [1973], Satterthwaite [1975]. Further encoding of
social choice problems into SAT led to results in a variety of settings Geist and Endriss [2011], Brandt and
Geist [2016], Brandt et al. [2017], Brandl et al. [2019, 2021]. Most relevant to our work is Geist [2016],
who encoded the search for CWS as a satisfiability problem. While they successfully identified minimal
elections with Condorcet dimension three, their approach faced significant computational hurdles. The SAT
encoding requires fixing the number of candidates and voters in advance, leading to poor scalability (the
biggest elections tested by Geist [2016] contain n = 12 voters).
More recently, Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) and mixed integer linear programming (MILP)
formulations have seen success in social choice, particularly in probabilistic settings Mennle and Seuken
[2016], Brandl et al. [2018]. Of particular relevance is the work of Berker et al. [2026], who successfully
applied MILP techniques to prove bounds on core stability in approval-based multi-winner voting.1 Our
automated reasoning approach in the ranked setting complements their work in the approval-based setting.
As they did, we relax the integrality of the voters, modeling the electorate as a probability distribution
over rankings. This allows us to search the space of elections with an effectively infinite number of voters,
removing the prior dependency of SAT-based approaches. We also take a similar approach to investigate the
relationship among different axioms.
Another successful application of MILP is in the field of automated mechanism design [Conitzer and
Sandholm, 2002, 2004]. In this setting, automated reasoning proved both impossibility and existence results,
such as for redistribution mechanisms [Guo and Conitzer, 2009, 2010, Damle et al., 2024].
1Since then, the existence of core-stable committees in that setting has been proven [Becker et al., 2026].

Preliminaries
A ranking-based election consists of m alternatives (or candidates) C = {c1, . . . , cm} and n voters N =
{1, . . . , n}. Each voter i ∈N has a strict ranking ≻i over C. In multi-winner ranked voting, the goal is
to select a committee W ⊂C of size k, where 0 < k < m. We denote the set of all such committees by
Mk = {W ⊆C | |W| = k}.
Definition 1 (α-undominated set). A set of alternatives W ⊆C is an α-undominated set if, for every
alternative c ∈C \ W, strictly less than an α fraction of the voters prefer c over every member of W.
A Condorcet winning set (CWS) is an (1/2)-undominated set.
To study the worst-case stability over any number of voters, we represent the electorate as a distribution
over rankings rather than enumerating individual voters as above. Let S be the set of all m! rankings of
the alternatives C. Every election induces a probability distribution x ∈∆(S), where x[s] is the fraction
of voters with the ranking s ∈S. Similarly, any voter distribution with rational inputs is induced by some
election with sufficiently many (integral) voters. For a ranking s, we write c ≻s c′ if alternative c is ranked
higher than c′ according to s and c ≻s W if alternative c is ranked higher than each c′ ∈W according
to s. One useful aspect of this formulation is that the fraction of voters who prefer an alternative c over a
committee W according to profile x is given by P

Beyond stability: connecting other axioms
Stability is just one property that we can use to measure the quality of a collective decision. We next study
the implications between stability and other notions of proportionality for ranked elections.
Rank-priceability [Brill and Peters, 2023] is an extension of priceability from the approval-based set-
ting [Peters and Skowron, 2020] to ranked preferences. In this setup, each voter is given an equal budget,

cutoff r, consider the voters who rank that alternative among their top r alternatives. Their collective un-
spent budget, together with their payments to elected alternatives outside their top r, must not exceed the
common price.

We focus on incomparability; we show that rank-priceability need not imply stability and, on the con-
trary, that stability need not imply priceability. Here, stability refers to the committee under consideration
being 2/(k + 1)-undominated. For each incomparability result we prove in this section, we present the
minimal (m, k) for which the implication breaks, meaning the implication holds true for all (m′, k′) such
that m′ < m or m′ = m and k′ < k.
To find a rank-priceable committee that is not α-undominated, we impose the payment constraints and
maximize an unelected alternative’s support against W. This is a linear program. For the reverse direction,
we use the dual of the linear program that determines whether W is rank-priceable. A feasible dual solution
with strictly positive objective certifies that W is not rank-priceable. We then minimize the largest unelected
alternative’s support subject to this certificate. The resulting program is bilinear. We give both formulations
in Section C.
In fact, the approach to prove incomparability is not unique to rank-priceability. In Section C, we give a
general approach to incorporate axioms defined by linear constraints into MILP to measure the implications
between axioms.
The programs produce voter profiles and payment or nonpriceability certificates from which we obtain
the following incomparability results.
Theorem 4. [Rank-priceability̸ ⇒stability] There exists a voter profile for m = 2 and k = 1 such that
there is a W ∈Mk that satisfies rank-priceability, but is not α-undominated for α = 1.
Despite being the smallest example showing separation between rank-priceability and 2/(k+1)- undom-
inatedness, this counterexample is degenerate since k = 1 and α = 1. We note that the smallest non-
degenerate counterexample (with k > 1 and α < 1) exists for m = 4 and k = 2. We also disprove the
implication in the other direction using an analogous program.
Theorem 5. [Stability̸ ⇒rank-priceability] There exists a voter profile for m = 3 and k = 2 such that
there is a W ∈Mk that is α-undominated for α = 2/3, but not rank-priceable.

be satisfied without hurting the worst-case stability guarantee?

Our results open several additional paths for future research. While we gave a regime for which a
2/(k+1)-undominated committee exists, it is still elusive whether this holds universally, and in particular if
a CWS of size three or four always exists. Scaling our formulations could reveal new counterexamples or
establish existence guarantees over larger parameter ranges. The resulting constructions and bounds may
also expose structural properties that lead to more general proofs.
The relationship between committee stability and the proportionality axioms studied here raises further
open questions. Although we established incomparability, imposing Rank-PJR+ does not change the optimal
stability guarantee for the parameters tested. Can Rank-PJR+ always be satisfied without sacrificing the
optimal worst-case stability guarantee? More broadly, which additional axioms do not change the optimal
worst-case stability guarantee? Answering these questions would shed light on the relationship between
proportional representation and committee stability.
Overall, our methods provide a systematic way to explore these questions through both counterexample
searches and existence certificates. Together with related automated reasoning approaches in approval-based
committee selection [Berker et al., 2026], our results demonstrate the value of optimization-based methods
for studying collective choice. We anticipate and hope that our techniques will be fruitful for tackling
additional problems in social choice.

a W ∈Mk that is α-undominated for α = 2/3, but does not satisfy Rank-EJR+.

First, W is 2/3-undominated. The only alternative not in W is alternative 2, and every voter ranks
committee member 1 above 2.
We now show that W violates Rank-EJR+. Take alternative c = 2, r = 2, and ℓ= 2. Every voter has
top2(s) = {1, 2}, so alternative 2 is in every voter’s top-2 alternatives, while
| top2(s) ∩W| = 1 < 2 = ℓ.
Since equality with the Rank-EJR+ quota is a violation, W does not satisfy Rank-EJR+.
