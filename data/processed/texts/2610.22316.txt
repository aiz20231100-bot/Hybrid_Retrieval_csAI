ABSTRACT
Large language model (LLM)-based agents and multi-agent systems increasingly tackle complex
tasks through long-horizon tool use and inter-agent collaboration. However, these systems are prone
to deviating from the overall goal as execution unfolds, which wastes execution resources and leads
to undesired outcomes, a failure termed goal drift. In this work, we propose a steering agent that
supervises execution on the fly and steers the system back on track when drift occurs. The steering
agent observes the execution context, deciding when to intervene with a low-cost fast path and how
to intervene with a high-cost slow path. To unlock its steering intelligence, the ability to assess
goal drift and generate effective interventions, we jointly train the two paths with reinforcement
learning, combining a pairwise ranking objective with group-relative policy optimization (GRPO).
Experiments on information-seeking search tasks show that the steering agent provides effective su-
pervision for both single-agent and multi-agent systems, improving their performance and efficiency.
The learned steering intelligence generalizes to out-of-domain tasks, transfers to unseen executors,
and supports controllable intervention frequency without retraining.

Introduction
Large language model (LLM)-based agents (Yao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Xi et al., 2025) and multi-agent sys-
tems (MAS) (Wu et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024) increasingly tackle complex tasks through long-horizon
tool use (Schick et al., 2023) and inter-agent collaboration (Hong et al., 2024), spanning information seeking (Team
et al., 2025), software engineering (OpenAI, 2025), and scientific discovery (Qu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026). How-
ever, these systems are prone to goal drift, where agents deviate from the overall goal as execution unfolds (Arike
et al., 2025; Cemri et al., 2025; Laban et al., 2026). A single agent may persist along an unproductive chain of actions,
while an agent in a MAS may prioritize its local objective over the collective goal. Goal drift can waste execution
resources and lead to undesired outcomes, even when individual actions appear locally reasonable (Cemri et al., 2025;
Liu et al., 2026a).
To keep agents on track and ensure goals are achieved during long-horizon execution, prevalent agent systems, such
as Claude Code (Anthropic, 2026a) and Codex (OpenAI, 2025), employ planning and verification (Young, 2025;

Anthropic, 2026b). As illustrated in Fig. 1, planning decomposes a goal into manageable subgoals (Wang et al.,
2023), but execution may still deviate from the planned route. Verification checks whether the goal has been achieved
before termination, but such post-hoc remediation can lead to irreversible consequences and costly retries. These
limitations motivate a natural design of on-the-fly supervision, where an external agent monitors execution and steers
the system back toward its goal when drift occurs, complementing planning and verification.
Such supervision is conceptually straightforward but challenging for the LLM backbone. It requires steering intelli-
gence, the ability to assess execution against the overall goal, determine when intervention is needed, and generate
actions that steer systems back on track. Such a capability is established in other goal-directed systems, such as di-
rect supervision in organizations (Mintzberg, 1979), supervisory control of automated systems (Sheridan, 1992), and
metacognitive monitoring and control in human cognition (Nelson & Narens, 1990). We bring this principle to LLM
agent systems by building a steering agent and improving its steering intelligence through post-training.
Specifically, we define an observation space that exposes the task goal and execution context of the agent system to
the steering agent. During execution, each observation update triggers a decision about whether and, if needed, how
to intervene. These two demands motivate a natural design with fast and slow paths (Kahneman, 2011; Su et al.,
2025; Lampson, 1983; Clark et al., 1989). The fast path estimates steering priority through a low-cost single-token
prediction. A threshold on this priority controls activation of the slow path, which leverages more costly reasoning to
generate steering actions. By separating fast judgment from deliberative generation, this design balances monitoring
efficiency and steering effectiveness. We then improve the steering intelligence of the steering agent by jointly training
the two paths with reinforcement learning, combining a pairwise ranking objective (Burges et al., 2005) for fast-path
steering judgment with group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) for slow-path action generation.
We evaluate our steering approach on information-seeking search tasks (Mialon et al., 2024; Ho et al., 2020) in both
single-agent and multi-agent settings. Our single-agent experiments show that steering is challenging even for a
capable LLM backbone, and that reinforcement learning substantially improves steering intelligence. Across seven
question-answering benchmarks, zero-shot steering yields a performance gain of only 1.84 points, whereas the RL-
optimized steering agent improves performance by 6.66 points with only 0.93 steering actions per task. Furthermore,
the steering intelligence generalizes to out-of-domain tasks, transfers to unseen executors, and supports controllable
intervention frequency without retraining, with steering at the highest frequency raising the gain to 17.11 points. In the
multi-agent setting, we introduce the steering agent into a five-role multi-agent system (Xu et al., 2026). In this more
challenging steering task, where zero-shot supervision degrades performance, the RL-trained steering agent achieves
a gain of 5.34 points.
Our contributions are summarized as follows.
• We characterize goal drift in single- and multi-agent systems, where local objectives can displace the overall task
goal, and motivate on-the-fly supervision to keep execution on track.
• We develop a steering agent with fast intervention judgment and deliberative action generation, and improve its
steering intelligence through reinforcement learning.
• We conduct experiments on both single- and multi-agent settings, where the steering agent demonstrates effective
performance gains, strong generalizability, transferability, and controllability.

Related Work
2.1
LLM-based Agent Systems
LLM-based single- and multi-agent systems are increasingly applied to complex, long-horizon tasks, including
information-seeking research (Manus, 2025; Team et al., 2025), software engineering (OpenAI, 2025; DeepSeek-
AI, 2026), and scientific discovery (Zhu et al., 2026; Qu et al., 2026). However, as increasingly complex tasks require
longer execution horizons, these systems may deviate from their original task objectives, a failure mode known as goal
drift (Arike et al., 2025; Cemri et al., 2025; Laban et al., 2026).
To better equip LLMs for long-horizon interaction and multi-agent collaboration, recent work has explored agentic
post-training, particularly reinforcement learning (RL) (Chen et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025). Long-
horizon agentic RL optimizes LLMs end-to-end with tools on complex tasks requiring extended interaction (Gao et al.,
2025; Team et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026). Multi-agent RL jointly optimizes collaborating agents under shared task-
level objectives, encouraging individual agents to contribute to overall task success (Liao et al., 2025; Zhao et al.,
2026). However, long-horizon rollouts can make training computationally expensive (Gao et al., 2025), and RL fine-
tuning can overfit to the training environment, affecting the generalization ability of the foundation model (Aissi et al.,

2024; Liu et al., 2026b; Lv et al., 2026). In contrast, we keep the task-executing LLMs frozen and train an external
agent to supervise execution and steer the system back toward its overall goal when drift occurs.
2.2
Goal Drift Mitigation
To mitigate goal drift during long-horizon execution, prevalent agent systems (DeepSeek-AI, 2026; Young, 2025;
OpenAI, 2025) employ planning and verification to support reliable execution. Planning decomposes a goal into
manageable subgoals that guide subsequent actions (Wang et al., 2023). Verification checks whether goals have been
met before an agent declares completion, prompting further execution or revision when needed (Anthropic, 2026b;
Lopopolo, 2026). However, a plan does not ensure reliable execution (Sinha et al., 2026), while verification may
uncover deviations after substantial effort has already been spent.
These limitations motivate a natural on-the-fly supervision design, where an external supervisor monitors intermediate
execution and intervenes when goal drift occurs. Recent work explores this idea with a primary focus on detection,
identifying when and where an agent goes wrong (Zhang et al., 2025; 2026; Baidya et al., 2026). SupervisorAgent (Lin
et al., 2026) takes a step toward correction by prompting an external LLM, but it targets easily fixable issues such as
errors and repetitive actions. Goal drift, however, is more challenging to detect and correct in real time, as it emerges
gradually from locally plausible actions. In this paper, we address this challenge with a steering agent that jointly
learns drift detection and correction through reinforcement learning.
3
Learning to Steer LLM-based Agent Systems
In this section, we begin by discussing goal drift that happens in both single-agent and multi-agent systems (Sec-
tion 3.1). Then, we introduce our steering agent design (Section 3.2) and the reinforcement learning algorithm (Sec-
tion 3.3).
3.1
Goal Drift in LLM-based Agent Systems
An LLM-based agent executes a task with an overall goal g by interacting with an environment (Yao et al., 2023). At
each step, the agent generates an action conditioned on g and the interaction history, and receives environment feed-
back. A multi-agent system distributes this process across specialized agents, where each agent handles a temporary
subgoal derived from g, such as retrieving evidence or verifying an answer, and hands its output to subsequent agents.
In both settings, execution unfolds as a sequence of temporary subgoals in service of the overall goal (March & Simon,
1958).
In LLM-based agent systems, goal drift appears when a temporary subgoal dominates the overall task goal (Arike
et al., 2025), leading to unproductive actions that might be locally reasonable. Goal drift reduces execution efficiency
by consuming resources on actions that contribute little to task completion. If the deviation remains uncorrected, the
agent system may never finish the task or finish it with an undesired outcome.
For example, in multi-hop search tasks (Ho et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2018), the agent needs to retrieve several distinct
pieces of evidence and combine them to answer a question, which involves a sequence of temporary retrieval subgoals.
A common failure mode is that the agent keeps searching over the same topic when key information has already been
obtained. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the first search already identifies the actress required to answer the question. The
agent spends three more searches on the same film instead of moving on to the next step, consistent with observed
patterns of redundant and unproductive search (Liu et al., 2026a).
In a multi-agent system, where specialized agents collaborate on a complex task, goal drift arises similarly when an
agent prioritizes its role objective over the overall task goal. A commonly observed failure mode is over-rejection,
where a verifier falsely rejects results that already satisfy the task goal (Huang et al., 2024; Stechly et al., 2025; Xu
et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 2(b), the verifier repeatedly rejects the answers the searchers have delivered, leading
execution to repeated revision rounds that can turn a successful execution into a failure. This reflects the verifier
overweighting its own goal of admitting only answers beyond doubt.

before further effort is wasted, achieving efficient goal completion.

step of a single agent enables fine-grained supervision, while exposing only inter-agent handoffs enables coarse-
grained supervision over a multi-agent system.
We formalize the steering process as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) (Sutton & Barto,
2018; Kaelbling et al., 1998), where the agent system being supervised acts as the environment. At decision step t, the
steering agent observes ot rather than the full execution state st, takes the action at, and the agent system transitions

As shown in Figure 3(a), each observation update marks a state transition in observation space and triggers a steering
decision. The steering agent then judges whether the current state needs intervention and generates the intervention
when it does. These two demands split the agent into a fast path and a slow path (Kahneman, 2011; Su et al., 2025;
Lampson, 1983; Clark et al., 1989). The fast path judges the need for intervention at low cost, and the slow path
produces the reasoning and action for intervention. Both paths share the same LLM backbone parameterized by θ.
Fast path.
To process each observation update at low cost, the fast path judges the need for intervention within a
single LLM forward pass. It operates by estimating the steering priority of the current state, which measures how
much a steering action at this state can change the final outcome. This allows the steering agent to achieve a larger
impact with a fixed intervention budget. Specifically, the fast path measures steering priority vθ(ot) by a yes/no
judgment, which is calculated by reading the probabilities of the two tokens:
zθ(ot) = log pθ(yes | Pfast(ot)) −log pθ(no | Pfast(ot)),

backbone, zθ(ot) is the log-odds between the two tokens, and the logistic function σ normalizes it to steering priority
vθ(ot) ∈(0, 1).

3.3
Reinforcement Learning for Steering Intelligence
Effective steering requires steering intelligence, the ability to assess execution against the overall goal, determine when
intervention is needed, and generate actions that bring execution back on track. To unlock this capability for the LLM
backbone, we conduct post-training on the steering interface, jointly optimizing the two paths with feedback from task
outcomes.
As illustrated in Figure 3(b), the reinforcement learning pipeline samples intermediate states that previous rollouts
have reached and lets the steering agent attempt interventions there. The steering agent generates several steering
actions at the sampled states, and their final rewards are compared together with those of the unsteered continuations.
The comparison teaches the fast path which states carry high steering priority through a pairwise ranking objective,
and teaches the slow path which actions improve outcomes through GRPO (Shao et al., 2024). Training keeps the
executor frozen and updates only the parameters θ of the steering agent.

Experiments
In this section, we conduct experiments to study three research questions. RQ1: Can a steering agent improve task per-
formance, and can reinforcement learning strengthen its steering intelligence? RQ2: Does the learned steering capa-
bility generalize to out-of-domain tasks, transfer to unseen executors, and support controllable intervention frequency?
RQ3: Can steering improve a multi-agent system, where goal drift arises from collaboration among specialized roles?
4.1
Experimental Setup
We follow the experimental setting of ASearcher (Gao et al., 2025), which trains on ASearcher-Base-35k and evaluates
on seven retrieval-augmented QA benchmarks of multi-hop reasoning and single-hop retrieval with a local Wikipedia
retrieval server. We report F1 (Rajpurkar et al., 2016) following the same protocol, with Avg. denoting the mean
over the seven benchmarks. For the executor, we use a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct search agent (Qwen et al., 2024), which
shows basic search ability but is prone to goal drift. Given the difficulty of the steering task, we use the stronger
Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025) as the backbone of the steering agent. Further details are described in Appendix A.
4.2
Effectiveness of Steering (RQ1)
To study the effectiveness of the steering agent, we compare it with planning and verification, which use the same
supervisor backbone (Qwen3-8B) to decompose the goal before execution and to review the goal completion afterward.
Table 1 reports F1 on the seven in-domain benchmarks, together with the corresponding steering cost and execution
cost, i.e., steering actions (#Act.) and executor turns (Turns) per task. We adjust the fast path threshold (Equation 4)
to keep the average number of steering actions around one per task for a fair comparison.
Zero-shot steering brings the largest gain among the three supervision forms and leaves executor turns nearly un-
changed. In comparison, zero-shot planning and verification help less or even degrade performance and lengthen
execution. These results show that intervening during execution supervises the executor more effectively than plan-
ning ahead or reviewing afterward.
To examine the improvement brought by reinforcement learning, we apply the same learning algorithm (Section 3.3)
to the steering, planning, and verification agents. RL improves all three supervision forms over their zero-shot coun-
terparts. Among them, steering benefits the most. RL raises the gain from 1.84 to 6.66 points and reaches the strongest
results consistently on all seven benchmarks, with the fewest steering actions and executor turns. These results show
that reinforcement learning effectively unlocks the steering intelligence, enabling the steering agent to mitigate goal
drift.
Regarding the learning algorithm, we additionally compare online reinforcement learning with an offline alternative,
where filtered behavior cloning (BC) (Chen et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2023) imitates the sampled steering actions that
improve the task outcome. Filtered BC falls behind our online algorithm, further validating the necessity of online
reinforcement learning.

4.3
Generalizability, Transferability, and Controllability (RQ2)
To study whether the learned steering intelligence generalizes to out-of-domain tasks, we further evaluate on tasks
that require an online search API, which exposes more complex real-world information. We test the same steering
model without additional training on three more challenging benchmarks that require deeper multi-hop search, namely
Frames (Krishna et al., 2025), GAIA (Mialon et al., 2024), and xBench-DeepSearch (Chen et al., 2025a) (Table 2).
Steering improves both Avg@4 and Pass@4 on Frames and GAIA, and raises Pass@4 on xBench-DeepSearch. These
results show that the learned steering capability generalizes beyond the training distribution. The steering agent mainly
learns to recognize and correct goal drift, while task-specific ability stays within the frozen executor, which mitigates
overfitting to the training tasks.
To test whether the steering intelligence learned with one execution model can transfer across different model families,
we use the same steering model to supervise three unseen executors, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024),
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Jiang et al., 2023) and Phi-4 (Abdin et al., 2024). As shown in Table 3, zero-shot steering
behaves consistently across the four model families and brings small gains. The steering agent trained with the Qwen
executor shows strong transferability, improving the three unseen executors by 15.78, 12.01, and 5.92 points while
effectively reducing the executor turns. These results show that the learned steering intelligence transfers to executors
unseen during training, allowing the executor to be freely switched without retraining.
We further examine the controllability of the intervention frequency by varying the threshold of Equation 4 at deploy-
ment without retraining. We visualize the total gain and the gain per intervention in Figure 4. In the left panel, RL
steering consistently improves task performance across all intervention budgets, surpassing zero-shot steering, Filtered
BC, and the planning and verification baselines. At the highest intervention frequency, RL steering raises the total gain
to 17.11 points. These results show that the fast path design turns intervention frequency into a deployment-time con-
trol, allowing the same model to trade steering cost for task performance. In the right panel, RL steering achieves
its largest gain per intervention at low intervention frequency, while zero-shot steering and filtered BC remain low
across frequencies, lacking the ability to judge where intervention matters most. This demonstrates that the fast path
learns to prioritize steering at the states that influence the final outcome most, yielding the largest gain under a fixed
intervention budget.

5
Conclusion
This paper targets goal drift in LLM-based agent systems and builds a steering agent that decides when to intervene
with a low-cost fast path and how with a deliberative slow path. Joint reinforcement learning over the two paths
unlocks steering intelligence that improves both single-agent and multi-agent systems, generalizes to out-of-domain
tasks, transfers to unseen executors, and supports controllable intervention frequency. As LLM-based agents and
multi-agent systems tackle increasingly complex and long-horizon tasks, the steering agent provides an effective on-
the-fly supervision tool to improve their performance and efficiency.

A
Implementation Details
A.1
Training Details
Single agent and multi-agent experiments share the same training setup. During training, the steering agent is initial-
ized from Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025). The backbone of the execution agent system is Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen
et al., 2024), which is served separately and excluded from parameter updates. We train with AdamW (Loshchilov &
Hutter, 2017) at a learning rate of 2 × 10−5 and a batch size of 64 tasks for 300 steps. The task reward R, first defined
in Section 3.2, is instantiated as answer token F1 multiplied by a trajectory-format indicator. The F1 term measures the
word-level overlap between the predicted and the reference answers, and the indicator is 1 when every executor turn
commits exactly one well-formed action tag and 0 otherwise. The remaining reinforcement learning hyperparameters
follow the defaults of the ASearcher framework.
Each task performs m = 2 selection rounds over the state bank, and each round selects up to k = 3 states. Each
selection takes a uniformly random state with probability εsel = 0.25 and otherwise the state with the highest steering
priority. Every selected state receives its full group of G = 4 steering actions. Each steered and unsteered state is
evaluated by N = 2 executor continuations, whose means give ¯Rs,j and the baseline bs in Equation 7. All new states
reached by the continuations are added to the state bank.
We train on ASearcher-Base-35k (Gao et al., 2025), a challenging QA dataset constructed from filtered open-source
multi-hop questions and agent-synthesized QA pairs. The training environment is the same local retrieval server as
the in-domain evaluation, using the 2018 Wikipedia dump (Karpukhin et al., 2020) as the information source and
E5 (Wang et al., 2022) as the retriever.
A.2
Evaluation Details
We follow ASearcher (Gao et al., 2025) to report token F1 on the in-domain benchmarks, and Avg. is the mean over
the seven benchmarks. Out-of-domain evaluation runs every question 4 times and reports Avg@4, the mean accuracy
over the four runs, and Pass@4, the fraction of questions solved in at least one run. We follow ASearcher to judge
answer correctness on these benchmarks with an LLM (Qwen3.8-max (Yang et al., 2025)) as judge, and the judge
prompt template is listed in Appendix C.
The in-domain evaluation data of ASearcher (Gao et al., 2025) includes seven retrieval-augmented QA benchmarks,
totaling 6,125 questions with 1,000 sampled questions from each benchmark and the full 125 questions of Bam-
boogle. 2WikiMultiHopQA (Ho et al., 2020) builds multi-hop questions from Wikipedia and Wikidata with annotated
reasoning paths. HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) contains two-hop questions that bridge or compare Wikipedia entities.
Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023) collects hand-written compositional questions that a standard search engine answers
incorrectly. MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022) composes questions of two to four hops from single-hop ones with stricter
connectivity requirements. Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) contains real search-engine queries answered
by Wikipedia. TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) contains trivia questions authored by quiz enthusiasts. PopQA (Mallen
et al., 2023) probes long-tail entity knowledge with templated questions built from Wikidata triples.
The out-of-domain evaluation uses three benchmarks that require deeper search over the open web. Frames (Krishna
et al., 2025) contains 824 questions that combine multiple constraints and typically require evidence from several
Wikipedia articles. GAIA (Mialon et al., 2024) is a general assistant benchmark, from which we evaluate the 103
text-only questions of its validation split. xBench-DeepSearch (Chen et al., 2025a) contains 100 questions targeting
the deep-search ability of agents. These evaluations use an online search engine.
A.3
Baseline Design
The planning, verification, and filtered behavior cloning baselines use the same supervisor backbone, and the trained
variants reuse the reinforcement learning algorithm, data, and hyperparameters for a fair comparison. The comparison
therefore isolates the supervision placement and the learning algorithm.
Planning.
The planning agent is triggered once before the executor’s first action. It reads the same task requirement
as the steering agent and writes a plan that the executor keeps in view for the whole task.
Verification.
The verification agent is triggered after the executor commits an answer and decides whether the sub-
mission stands. It either passes the answer or rejects it with feedback. The feedback is read by the executor agent as a
tool-return, and the executor revises and resubmits. After three rejections the latest answer is delivered without further
review.

Filtered behavior cloning.
The filtered BC baseline (Wang et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021) collects the steering
actions sampled by the same rollout pipeline as our online RL algorithm and keeps those whose mean reward ¯Rs,j
exceeds the unsteered baseline bs in Equation 7. Then it treats these positive samples as the training data for supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) (Ouyang et al., 2022). The retained pairs of observation and steering action fine-tune the steering
agent with a token-level cross-entropy objective (Zelikman et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2023). Compared with our online
algorithm, filtered BC is an offline algorithm, so it receives no signal from the discarded negative samples and its
training data does not track the improving policy.
A.4
Multi-Agent Instantiation
The multi-agent system adapts the five-role template of TacoMAS (Xu et al., 2026) to search tasks. A planner de-
composes the question into subgoals and dispatches them to two searchers, which retrieve evidence in parallel. A
verifier reviews the searchers’ deliverables against the question, and a summarizer composes the final answer from the
delivered evidence and the verifier’s verdict.
The steering agent reviews a pending handoff using the task, the multi-agent structure, previously delivered handoffs,
current handoff, its sender and recipients, and any rejection feedback already issued at that handoff. The action space
contains a pass that delivers the handoff unchanged, a note that attaches guidance to the delivered submission for its
recipients, and a retry that returns rejection feedback to the sender for revision and resubmission. The full prompt
templates are listed in Appendix C.

Hyperparameters
To study the impact of hyperparameters, we retrain the steering agent varying the number of selection rounds m, the

all configurations deliver comparable gains at the 15% frequency, staying within a band of one F1 point. When the

B.3
LLM-as-a-Judge Results on In-Domain Benchmarks
To examine whether the in-domain results depend on the token F1 metric, we follow ASearcher (Gao et al., 2025) to
supplement LLM-as-a-Judge (LasJ) accuracy for all methods in Table 1. The judge model and prompt template are
the same as in the out-of-domain evaluation (Appendix A.2). As shown in Table 5, LasJ accuracy exhibits the same
trend as Table 1. The RL improved steering agent achieves the best results consistently on all seven benchmarks, and
RL raises the gain of steering from 1.60 to 6.77 points. LasJ accuracy is higher than token F1 overall, as the judge
accepts answers that match the reference in meaning but differ in surface form.
