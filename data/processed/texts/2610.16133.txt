Query Your Way Out: Benchmarking Multi-Turn Knowledge
Reasoning through Generalized Puzzle Solving
Xin Cheng 1 Junming Yang 1 Ning Xu 1
Abstract
Puzzles offer a natural testbed for Large Language
Models (LLMs) reasoning because they pose ver-
ifiable goals under partial information and require
multi-step decisions to reach a solution. However,
existing puzzle benchmarks typically focus on
narrow environments with rigid mechanics and
tightly constrained solution targets. To bridge
this gap, we introduce the Active Probing Bench-
mark (APB), which generalizes puzzle-solving by
casting concept identification as an interactive rea-
soning task that integrates specialized knowledge
assessment into the puzzle setting. Specifically,
APB evaluates whether models can acquire infor-
mation via multi-turn active probing and inference
to pinpoint 960 target concepts of specialized dif-
ficulty across eight diverse academic domains.
Our evaluation reveals a counter-intuitive phe-
nomenon: most open-source reasoning models
significantly underperform their standard instruc-
tion counterparts. This finding suggests that in
interactive settings, these models struggle to lever-
age procedural reasoning to reliably access and
apply specialized domain knowledge. Beyond
these empirical insights, APB provides a scalable
pipeline for generating benchmarks that probe
knowledge reasoning under interaction across di-
verse settings.
1. Introduction
The rapid progress of Large Language Models (LLMs) has
heightened the demand for evaluation paradigms that re-
liably reflect underlying model capabilities (Singh et al.,
2024). Although existing benchmarks have enabled system-
atic comparison, their validity is increasingly constrained by
practical challenges in benchmark design and upkeep. Prior
work has noted that overlap between evaluation data and

large-scale pre-training corpora may confound performance
estimates in some settings (Li et al., 2023; Jacovi et al.,
2023; Roberts et al., 2023), motivating interest in evaluation
protocols that emphasize reasoning over memorization (Xu
et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). Dynamic benchmarks have
emerged as one response (Kiela et al., 2021; Shirali et al.,
2022), including continuously updated datasets such as Lat-
estEval (Li et al., 2024) and LiveBench (White et al., 2024).
However, such approaches depend on sustained data cura-
tion and may be difficult to scale. Other paradigms, such as
crowdsourced pairwise comparison in Chatbot Arena (Chi-
ang et al., 2024), introduce variability driven by subjective or
stylistic factors. Meanwhile, agent-based benchmarks (Liu
et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2024) primarily
target domains with explicit verification signals, leaving
the evaluation of general knowledge reasoning relatively
underexplored.
Puzzle tasks have become a widely used evaluation
paradigm for LLMs. With clearly defined objectives and
verifiable endpoints, they enable assessment not only of
outcomes but also of trajectory-level signals of exploratory
reasoning (Giadikiaroglou et al., 2024). Prior work has of-
ten focused on specific classes of puzzles (Chollet, 2019;
Lin et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025), including riddles, Su-
doku, and related logic or combinatorial games. Recent
benchmarks have advanced this approach. For instance,
SPLAT (Chen et al., 2024) frames tasks as clue-seeking
within pre-scripted narratives, and MTP (Badola et al., 2025)
assesses models through carefully designed interactive en-
vironments. However, such environments typically define
narrow action spaces that encourage models to depend on
fixed strategies rather than engaging in deep knowledge rea-
soning. Moreover, the highly constrained goals of these
scenarios restrict the evaluation method from being gener-
alized to broader contexts. Our work extends the use of
puzzle-solving by integrating multi-turn active probing with
domain knowledge, aiming instead to bridge this gap and
evaluate whether models can demonstrate a general under-
standing of any established concept.

(APB), which expands the scope of puzzle-solving eval-

Additionally, open source reasoning models tend to commit
to specific candidate targets early and then probe in a confir-
matory manner, leading to inefficient probing when the true
target lies outside the initial hypothesis set. These analysis
suggests that in interactive settings, the models struggle to
leverage procedural reasoning to reliably access and apply
specialized domain knowledge.
Beyond these empirical findings, the construction of the
APB introduces a scalable method to rapidly generate diag-
nostic benchmarks in specific specialized domain, enabling
a robust evaluation of knowledge reasoning. We further
showcase its application by building an IC Back-End Puzzle
Benchmark, featuring 480 puzzles from four EDA com-
mand tools in the IC back-end design (Appendix A). The
core contribution can be summarized as follows:
• We generalize the paradigm of puzzle-solving assess-
ment by expanding the action and target spaces, shifting
the focus from game mechanics to general knowledge
reasoning across broader domains.
• We introduce the Active Probing Benchmark (APB) and
a scalable pipeline for generating evaluations of spe-
cialized difficulty, instantiated by 960 puzzles derived
from expert-level concepts.
• We identify a critical proficiency gap in leading open-
source reasoning models, observing that under the in-
teractive evaluation paradigm of APB, these models
struggle to leverage procedural reasoning to reliably
access and apply specialized domain knowledge.

2. Related Work
2.1. Benchmark Evaluation
The evaluation of large language models has been largely
driven by a suite of comprehensive benchmarks designed
to assess their knowledge and reasoning abilities across
a multitude of domains (Cobbe et al., 2021; Liu et al.,
2024b; Rein et al., 2024; Clark et al., 2018; Hendrycks et al.,
2021). Prominent examples such as MMLU (Hendrycks
et al., 2020), BIG-Bench (Srivastava et al., 2023) and Hel-
laSwag (Zellers et al., 2019) have become standard tools for
measuring model performance (Contributors, 2023; Duan
et al., 2024). A common characteristic of these benchmarks
is their reliance on static, single-turn formats, typically in-
volving multiple-choice questions or direct answer gener-
ation (Wang et al., 2024b; Suzgun et al., 2022; Yue et al.,
2024; Lu et al., 2022). However, static benchmarks are vul-
nerable to data leakage, since portions of their test data may
inadvertently be included in the massive datasets used for
model pre-training (Balloccu et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024;
Ni et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023). One way to mitigate cheat-
ing is through dynamically updated benchmarks, such as
LatestEval (Li et al., 2024) and LiveBench (White et al.,
2024), which continuously incorporate the latest real-world
data. While this approach alleviates the issue to some ex-
tent, it still fails to enable fine-grained analysis and precise
evaluation of the internal knowledge a model has acquired.
To address this, we propose a dynamic, multi-turn frame-
work designed to preclude rote memorization and compel
the model to externalize its deductive reasoning.
2.2. Puzzles and Games as Evaluation Paradigms
Puzzles, broadly defined as structured problems that require
reasoning to reach a solution, have long served as effective
probes of intelligence (Guo et al., 2024; Estermann et al.,
2024; Shah et al., 2024). They constrain the search space
while still demanding creativity, logical inference, and the
integration of disparate knowledge. Accordingly, puzzles
and games have been widely adopted as formal evaluation
paradigms, from classic board games like Chess and Go (Sil-
ver et al., 2017) to text-based adventure games (Wang et al.,
2024a; Gallotta et al., 2024; Zhang & Long, 2025) and in-
teractive formats such as 20 Questions (Vinyals et al., 2019;
Liang et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2018). A common feature of
these settings is sustained, multi-turn interaction within a
structured environment, which makes them well-suited for
assessing strategic planning, logical deduction, and efficient
use of information (Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Chen
et al., 2025). Motivated by this tradition, we frame evalua-
tion as a multi-turn process that requires a model to external-
ize its reasoning trajectory rather than merely produce final
answers. The Active Probing framework repurposes the
puzzle format to evaluate how a model retrieves, integrates,

3. Method
ACTIVE PROBING BENCHMARK is founded on a fundamen-
tal generalization of the puzzle definition, which enables
the rapid construction of custom reasoning benchmarks.
Section 3.1 details this generalized puzzle setting, along
with our evaluation settings and metrics. Section 3.2 then
describes the construction details in the APB.
3.1. Multi-turn Active Probing Process
Our evaluation method is a multi-turn interactive protocol.
Its extensibility comes from a general definition of a puz-
zle as target concept identification through active evidence
acquisition.
Generalized Puzzle Definition.
Each instance specifies a
hidden target concept Ptarget (e.g., a specialized Wikipedia
title or a technical command) and an associated reference
document Iref that is accessible only to the oracle. The
model must identify Ptarget by asking questions and integrat-
ing turn-level feedback rather than by reading Iref directly.
Problem Formulation. Let V denote the concept space and
initialize the candidate set as S0 = V . The model interacts
for at most T = MAX ROUNDS turns. At each turn t, the
model produces a multiple-choice query Qt = (qt, Ot) and
options Qt = (qt, Ot) including question qt and options
Ot, and receives feedback At. The interaction history is
Ht = {(Q1, A1), . . . , (Qt, At)}.
Interaction Mechanism. Algorithm 1 summarizes the inter-
action loop. At each turn t, the Querying Agent conditions
on the accumulated history Ht−1 and outputs a multiple-
choice query Qt = (qt, Ot). If Ptarget ∈Qt, the episode ter-
minates with SUCCESS; otherwise, the Knowledge Oracle
consults the reference Iref and returns turn-level validation
feedback At = V(Qt, Iref) without exposing the reference
text. The pair (Qt, At) is appended to form Ht for the next
turn, and the process continues until success or the budget
T is exhausted.

and then progressively concentrates on concepts that require

of the following four key stages:
• Stage 1 (Broad filter): We rapidly removed common
entities, pop culture references, and general knowledge
terms, yielding an initial pool of candidates.
• Stage 2 (Expertise filter): We applied prompt based crite-
ria that retain only titles judged to require doctorate level
specialized knowledge claimed in prompts, discarding
undergraduate or introductory concepts.
• Stage 3 (Domain stratification): The remaining candi-
dates were categorized into eight academic domains to
ensure balanced coverage (e.g., biology, physics, humani-
ties, and health sciences).
• Stage 4 (Difficulty selection): Within each domain, we
ran a dynamic tournament style selection: from batches of
roughly 150 specialized candidates, we retained only the
5–10 most challenging items according to model based
difficulty scoring. This step concentrates the final bench-
mark on the extreme tail of the difficulty distribution.
Across stages, the pipeline retains approximately 0.14%
of the original title set, producing a pool of roughly 9k
advanced candidates and randomly selecting 120 targets
per domain (960 puzzles total). The filtering code and
prompts are released for reproducibility.1 For each puzzle,
the cleaned text from the corresponding Wikipedia article
serves as the reference document Iref.

3.3. The Active Probing Benchmark
Benchmark Composition.
The Active Probing Bench-
mark is composed of 960 puzzles, which are systematically
categorized into eight distinct domains: Biology, Human
Disease, Chemistry, Physics, Mathematics, Computer Sci-
ence, Economics, and Philosophy. These domains were
chosen to ensure comprehensive coverage of major aca-
demic fields, including the humanities and social sciences,
the natural and engineering sciences, and health.
Each domain comprises 120 puzzles distributed across 7
to 8 primary sub-disciplines (Figure 4) to ensure diverse
topical coverage. For example, the Physics domain sources
items from eight distinct subfields, with key contributions
from Quantum Field Theory and Mathematical Physics. We
also provide additional granularity within each subfield. For
instance, the ”Specialized Topics” sub-discipline covers
Plasma Physics, Atomic, Molecular, and Optical (AMO)
Physics, and Particle and Nuclear Physics. This multi-topic
setup is a fundamental aspect of our benchmark across all
domains, supporting a comprehensive evaluation of expert
knowledge. Full details are described in the Appendix D.

4.2. Main Results
The main evaluation results are presented in Table 2, cov-
ering detailed success rate assessments across 8 domains:
Biology (Bio), Human Disease (Dis), Chemistry (Chem),
Physics (Phys), Mathematics (Math), Computer Science
(CS), Economics (Econ), and Philosophy (Phil).
Analysis of Main Results. The evaluation results demon-
strate pronounced disparities across 8 domains (Table 2). In
the strongest-performing domains, Human Disease (27.8%)
and Economics (23.2%) achieved the highest average suc-
cess rates. The high accuracy in Human Disease can be
attributed to its comprehensive and well-structured classifi-
cation system, while the strong performance in Economics
may stem from the relatively lower depth and complexity
of its specialized knowledge. In the most challenging do-
mains, Mathematics (11.5%) and Computer Science (11.6%)
recorded the lowest success rates, where even GPT-5, the
strongest model, attained only 34.2% and 25.0% respec-
tively. Intermediate performance was observed in Biology,
Chemistry, Physics, and Philosophy.
From a model-centric perspective, the results reveal a clear
performance hierarchy. While GPT-5 leads the pack, its
predecessor GPT-4o (19.6%) demonstrates remarkable

Probing Length. The Probing Length (PL) measures the
average character length of the agent’s output per turn. This
output typically includes not only the multiple-choice ques-
tion but also the model’s explicit reasoning based on prior
rounds. Our analysis reveals that increased verbosity does
not correlate with better performance on this task. Indeed,
efficiency in knowledge probing rounds appears to be a
hallmark of the top models like GPT-5 (PL = 300). This
trend generally holds for the other top-performing models
with relatively concise probing lengths. Furthermore, we
observe that PL is often strongly correlated with a model’s
developer family, suggesting that different series of models
possess inherent and consistent styles for expressing their
reasoning process in this interactive setting. For instance,
both Gemini models exhibit a similarly high verbosity (PL
≈907), as do the DeepSeek models (PL ≈560) and the
OpenAI models (299 < PL < 500). This suggests that
different series of models possess inherent and consistent
styles for expressing their probing process in this interactive
setting.
Incorrect Guess Rounds. The Incorrect Guess Rounds
(IG) metric quantifies the average number of times an agent
makes a conclusive but incorrect guess of the target concept
per session. We interpret a high IG as an indicator of model
overconfidence, where the agent prematurely commits to a
wrong answer despite having insufficient information. Our
results show a strong negative correlation between IG and
Success Rate, suggesting that early incorrect commitments
are associated with persistent mislocalization in the knowl-
edge space, which limits the usefulness of subsequent prob-
ing. For instance, the top-performing model GPT-5 has the
lowest IG (0.32), while the struggling DeepSeek R1 has
the highest (5.13). From a strategic standpoint, such actions
represent a low-utility approach by invalidating only a single
hypothesis. In a context like Human Disease, a propensity
for making frequent, incorrect assertions is a harmful failure
mode that could lead to detrimental outcomes, underscoring
the importance of penalizing such overconfident behavior
in model evaluation.
Incorrect Probing and Error Correction. We analyze two
metrics related to probing errors: Incorrect Option rounds
(IO) and Incorrect Streak rounds (IS). IO measures the aver-
age number of rounds where all four proposed options are
incorrect, suggesting inaccurate concept grounding during
knowledge localization. IS measures the longest consecu-
tive sequence of such rounds, reflecting the model’s ability
to recover from a flawed reasoning path. However, these
metrics are not simple indicators of poor performance. An
’all-incorrect’ round can be highly informative for a capa-
ble agent, as it allows for significant pruning of the search
space and a major strategy correction. This can paradoxi-
cally help a strong model quickly locate an obscure concept.
Thus, these metrics offer insights into a model’s strategic
behavior. The case of DeepSeek R1 is particularly illus-
trative: despite having the lowest IO (3.63) and IS (2.22),
it achieves one of the lowest success rates. This suggests
that its probing strategy is ineffective at leveraging infor-
mation and asking questions to find the Ptarget, a strategic
failure that helps explain its high rate of premature guesses,
as reflected in its top-ranking IG.
Overall Efficiency. We evaluate efficiency using two com-
plementary metrics: Average Success Rounds (SR) and
Average Total Rounds (TR). SR measures the average num-
ber of rounds in successful sessions only, reflecting speed on
solvable problems. TR provides a more holistic score by in-
cluding failed sessions, which are counted as MAX ROUNDS
(15), thus blending success rate with efficiency. Relying
solely on SR can be misleading for models with low success
rates, as it only reflects performance on easier tasks they
could solve. TR, therefore, offers a more comprehensive
view. For example, GPT-5’s top performance gives it the
best TR (13.02) and leading SR (9.15), confirming its po-
sition as the most effective model overall. This combined
metric also reveals nuances in reasoning efficiency. For
instance, despite Claude Opus 4 having a higher Suc-

4.4. Error Analysis
Open-Source Reasoning Models.
To understand the
relatively weak performance of open source reasoning
models, we analyzed the publicly available chain-of-
thought traces for GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek R1, and
Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking. We observe a recur-
ring pattern that is best characterized as a failure of knowl-
edge localization during active probing. Rather than using
early turns to acquire broadly discriminative evidence, these
models often anchor on several concrete candidate targets
for Ptarget at the beginning of an episode (Figure 5). Subse-
quent multiple-choice queries are then constructed to seek
confirmation for these candidates, instead of eliciting infor-
mation that would re-partition the broader candidate space.
This premature anchoring has two downstream effects. First,
when the true Ptarget is not among the initial hypotheses, the
model’s questions tend to target attributes that are irrelevant
to the correct region of the domain knowledge space, yield-
ing feedback that is weakly informative for correction. As
a result, the interaction trace exhibits repetitive cycles of
querying around the wrong neighborhood, with many low-
utility terms and little reduction of the remaining uncertainty.
Second, the same anchoring increases the likelihood of con-
clusive but unsupported commitments, producing more fre-
quent incorrect final guesses. This is consistent with the
comparatively high IG values observed for these models and
with their lower success rates overall.
These traces suggest that the performance gap is driven
less by a lack of procedural reasoning steps and more by
difficulty in using multi-turn feedback to maintain and up-
date a well-grounded hypothesis set. In other words, the
models often fail to treat early turns as evidence acquisition
for concept identification, and instead collapse prematurely
onto specific candidates, which limits their ability to recover
when the initial localization is wrong.
Gemini Series Models. The Gemini 2.5 Pro models
exhibit a distinct behavioral pattern that helps explain their
relatively weak performance. A primary characteristic is
their high Probing Length (PL ≈907), far exceeding other
top closed-source models. We observe that this verbosity
stems from a tendency to repeatedly summarize and elab-
orate on prior turns before issuing the next query. From a
knowledge probing perspective, this behavior shifts effort
from acquiring new discriminative evidence to reprocessing
self-generated context, which can dilute the influence of
objective turn-level feedback. As a result, the model may
persist in a mislocalized hypothesis set and continue probing
within an irrelevant region of the knowledge space, culmi-

5. Conclusion
We introduced APB, an interactive benchmark that extends
puzzle evaluation from narrow game-like settings to knowl-
edge reasoning over specialized concepts. By casting con-
cept identification as multi-turn information seeking with
verifiable endpoints, APB provides process-level evidence
of how models acquire and use domain knowledge. Exper-
iments show that APB is challenging even for strong sys-
tems (GPT-5 reaches 33.4% success), and that several open
source reasoning models lag behind their no-thinking coun-
terparts, consistent with difficulties in grounding multi-turn
inference in specialized knowledge. Beyond these results,
APB offers a practical construction pipeline for producing
challenging diagnostic evaluations in additional specialized
domains, helping broaden the empirical study of knowledge
reasoning under interaction.

A. Rapid Custom Benchmark Construction
Our framework provides a scalable and generalizable
method for rapidly constructing diagnostic benchmarks in
any specialized domain. The process is straightforward and
consists of two main stages:
• Target Identification and Curation: First, a domain
of interest and a corresponding source corpus of knowl-
edge are identified (e.g., technical manuals, API doc-
umentation, academic articles). From this corpus, a
list of key concepts, terms, commands, or entities is
systematically extracted to serve as the puzzle targets
(Ptarget)
• Reference Document Generation: For each puzzle tar-
get, a ”golden” reference document (Iref) is established
(e.g., the specific documentation page for a command).
This document serves as the ground truth for the envi-
ronment’s feedback during the multi-turn evaluation.

To demonstrate the efficacy of this method, we applied it
to the niche, industrial domain of IC back-end design. We
used the official documentation for four widely-used EDA
command sets—Synopsys ICC, ICC2, and Cadence Innovus
(both common and legacy)—as our source corpus.

A.2. Broader Applications and Future Directions
The methodology detailed in this section is designed for
broad applicability beyond the technical domains demon-
strated. We encourage the research community to leverage
our scalable process to construct new benchmarks across a
wide array of fields. For example, puzzle targets could be
drawn from the humanities (e.g., famous figures, artworks,
film titles) or the natural sciences (e.g., celestial bodies,
materials, biological species).
The creation of such diverse benchmarks would facilitate
a more holistic evaluation of an LLM’s all-around knowl-
edge reasoning abilities. Moreover, the resulting multi-turn
interaction data would provide a rich resource for deeper,
cross-domain pattern recognition, enabling a more com-
prehensive exploration of model reasoning strategies and
behaviors.

Our case

differences between judges: context robustness and seman-

• Grok-4’s Outlier Performance (Context Robustness):

the most dramatic improvement (+11.5%) under the
stronger judge.
• Judging Characteristics (Association vs.
Preci-
sion): DeepSeek V3 tends toward associative valida-
tion—occasionally accepting highly related “attributes”
as the target “entity” itself. In contrast, Claude Sonnet 4
enforces stricter semantic precision.

Scaling Laws of Test-Time Compute
We further investi-
gated the relationship between reasoning effort and perfor-
mance using GPT-5. By varying the inference budget across
Low, Medium (Default), and High settings, we observed
a distinct inverted-U trajectory: performance peaks at
Medium effort (38.3%) and declines at High effort (34.6%).
This observation leads to two critical insights:
1. Diminishing Returns: In the constrained paradigm
of strategic questioning, excessive test-time compute
does not linearly translate to better outcomes. ”Over-
thinking” may lead to hallucinated constraints or overly
convoluted search paths.
2. Strategy Quality over Quantity: Crucially, even at the
“Low” effort setting, GPT-5 (33.3%) significantly out-
performs the best open-source reasoning models. This
suggests that the quality of the underlying reasoning
strategy—how the model prunes the search space—is a
more determinant factor than the raw quantity of infer-
ence steps or token generation.
C. Token Usage
This section details the token consumption patterns, aver-
aged per puzzle, for both the Querying Agent (QA) and the
Knowledge Oracle (KO). It is important to note that a single
puzzle evaluation consists of multiple rounds of interaction.
The token cost of the Knowledge Oracle (KO) is character-
ized by highly efficient outputs but demanding inputs. The
KO’s completion tokens are minimal and stable across all
tests (averaging 460 tokens), as its responses are restricted to
a fixed format providing only correctness judgments. This
ensures a fair and consistent evaluation. However, its prompt
token requirements are substantial, averaging 18,347 tokens
per puzzle, because the KO must process the full reference
document (Iref) in each round to provide accurate feedback.
Similarly, the QA prompt costs are significant, averaging
18,826 tokens per puzzle, as the full interaction history is in-
cluded in the context for each new turn. The vast majority of
performance variance and economic cost, however, comes
from the SA’s completion tokens, which reflect model ver-
bosity (Table 6). Notably, some of the poorest-performing
open-source reasoning models were paradoxically the most
computationally expensive. GPT-OSS-120B was the most
costly, consuming over 72,000 total QA tokens on average,
followed by Qwen3 235B Thinking (approx. 57,600 tokens)
and DeepSeek R1 (approx. 43,300 tokens). Collectively,
these three open-source models averaged 57,784 QA to-
kens per puzzle—almost exactly double the 28,705-token
average of the remaining models. This contrasts sharply
with top-performing models like GPT-5, which achieved the
highest success rate while using less than half the tokens of
GPT-OSS-120B. This suggests that excessive verbosity is
not only an unsuccessful strategy but also an economically
inefficient one for this task.

E. Active Probing Prompt
Querying Agent Prompt
You are a {domain} expert. I will have a preset {domain} name/term/concept in mind, and you need to continuously
ask questions and reason until you can identify the name/term/concept. You can ask questions to narrow down the
possibilities. Your questions must be in the form of a single, four-option multiple-choice question at a time.
You can only ask one multiple-choice question at a time. Each question is crucial and should be based on your
reasoning, professional knowledge, and the previous rounds of questions and answers. Please try to identify the
