Abstract
A real-time auditory scene analyzer (ASA) aims to carry out the tasks of locating,
separating and classifying the sound sources present in a given acoustic environ-
ment. Recently, an effort has been made into modelling an ASA as a multi-agent
system, with each one of its agents performing one of the aforementioned tasks
and communicating their results to the rest of their peer agents. These commu-
nication routes are used as feedback loops to fix local errors at a global level,
providing robustness while reducing local complexity. An example of the benefits
of this approach is the optimization of speech quality by correcting in real-time
the estimated location of the speech source of interest. However, their optimiza-
tion speed has been shown to be considerably slow. One possible reason is that it
solely relies on a series of single quality estimations (provided by a reference-free
quality estimator model) that vary considerably from one window to the next,
which results in a difficult search space to optimize. In this work, a new opti-
mization mechanism is proposed that instead relies on a series of sets of quality
estimations over a range of locations, providing a clearer view of the search space,
simplifying its optimization. The proposed ASA now has a considerably smaller
optimization time, is more accurate, and is more stable when being evaluated in
real-life acoustic scenarios to correct higher levels of localization errors, all while
being less complex than previous efforts. The only trade-off is that there is an
increase in the response time of the quality estimation agent, but the complete
ASA is still able to run in real-time. The performance shown in this work again
shows the benefits of modelling an ASA as a multi-agent system.
Keywords: auditory scene analysis, real-time, speech quality, optimization time

1 Introduction
A real-time auditory scene analyzer (ASA) aims to carry out, and in conjunction,
the tasks of locating, separating, and classifying sound sources in a given acoustic
environment [1, 2]. Doing so can provide important benefits to application areas such
as bioacoustics [3], hearing aid research [4, 5], search and rescue scenarios [6], human-
robot interaction [7], etc.
Usually, in an ASA, the aforementioned tasks are carried out in a linear manner
[8, 9]: all the sound sources are located; then, using their locations, each is separated
into its own channel; and, once separated, information is extracted from each source.
However, doing so makes the whole ASA sensitive to localization errors (the first task),
which begets the development of more complex localization techniques that are more
robust [10]. This, in turn, increases the computational load and the global response
time. Thus, carrying out these tasks in a linear manner results in local task-level errors
having a global impact.
To counter this issue, recently, there has been an effort [11, 12] to model an ASA as
a multi-agent system, where each agent is in charge of a particular task (localization,
separation or classification) and sends its results to other agents as a type of feedback.
This inter-agent interaction has been shown to provide local error robustness, while
reducing overall global complexity. The current version of the multi-agent auditory
scene analyzer (MASA) [12] is shown in Figure 1.

As it can be seen, there are several communication loops between agents. The
focus of this work is the feedback loop between the agents in charge of the separation
tasks (named beamformphase and demucs) and the result of the localization task
(θest being outputted by the soundloc agent). There are two other agents that are
part of this loop: the quality estimation agent (onlinesqa) and the optimization agent
(doaoptimizer). The aim of this feedback loop is to find the location (θcorr) that
optimizes the separation quality (Q). It has shown to be able to globally correct
localization errors at the local agent-level in real-time [11], reducing the need of highly
2


complex localization techniques [10]. Later, the work in [12] improves the optimization
process with which the localization error is corrected by introducing ‘memory’ of the
best location it has found. It provides much more robust results, with varying degrees
of success in different acoustic scenarios [12].
In both works [11, 12], the optimization process relies on a series of single quality
estimations provided by a reference-free model called Squim [13], while an Adam-based
optimizer [14] is employed to carry out the optimization process. However, in both
works, the optimization mechanism requires a long time to reach the correct location,
while providing high variability (low stability) in its location correction. Additionally,
the range of localization errors that both mechanisms can reliably correct is relatively
small, while being moderately complex.
In this work, a new optimization mechanism is proposed that relies, instead, on
a series of sets of quality estimations at each optimization iteration. Each quality
estimation inside each set is ‘steered’ towards different locations, providing a clearer
view of the search space. This, in turn, simplifies the optimization process to a simple
weighted average, using the quality estimation values as weights. From these two simple
operators being carried out in both the quality estimation agent and the optimization
agent, an optimization process emerges that not only provides much more robust and
stable results than the previous optimization mechanisms, but is able to correct a
wider range of localization errors, with smaller optimization times, all while being
less complex. This new optimization mechanism is further evidence of the benefits of
modeling an ASA as a multi-agent system where, by dealing with local errors globally,
robust results are obtained with less overall complexity.
This work is structured as follows: Section 2 provides background on modeling an
auditory scene analyzer as a multi-agent system, as well as details of the previous
optimization mechanisms for correcting localization errors through quality feedback;
Section 3 describes the proposed optimization mechanism; Section 4 details the evalua-
tion material and methodology, presents the results of the parameter characterization,
and provides a comparison with the previous optimization mechanisms; and Section
5 presents the concluding remarks as well as future work.
2 Background of Multi-agent Auditory Scene Analysis
Recently, carrying out the tasks of locating, separating and classifying the sound
sources in the environment in conjunction has been modeled as a multi-agent system
[11, 12], with each agent carrying out a different task in a non-linear manner. This
approach has shown to be globally robust against agent-level errors, with an initial
study correcting localization errors through the feedback of the separated speech qual-
ity [11]. The target speech is separated by using its location through a pre-beamforming
stage; thus, if the location is incorrect, the speech quality degrades. The main idea
is to correct in real-time this location by optimizing the speech quality, as shown in
Figure 2.

compared to the optimization mechanism presented earlier in [11]. However, it does
not provide any reduction in optimization time and is more complex (although it is
still able to run in real-time).
It is essential to note that, even though Squim’s response time makes it viable to
be run in real-time, it was not built to be run in a causal manner: it assumes that
it is being fed a complete audio recording. In the real-time scenario presented in this
work, a series of audio windows are fed instead. To this effect, it has been shown [11]
that Squim estimates a quality from each audio window that is different from the rest
of the windows of the series, even when the true quality of all windows is the same.
This results in a series of single quality estimations that vary considerably through
time, creating a very difficult search space to optimize for an Adam-based technique.
Thus, low update factors end up being required to find the correct location, but do
so in a very slow manner. In this work, it is hypothesized that a clearer view of the
search space for each window (ie. optimization iteration) can simplify the optimization
process, as it is discussed in the following section.
3 Proposed Optimization Mechanism
Instead of relying on a series of single quality estimations that vary through time, in
this work, a new optimization mechanism is proposed where a series of sets of quality
estimations are carried out based on the outputs of a set of beamformed locations. To
this effect, the quality is not only estimated from the requested location (θmid), but
also from a number of locations (θsteps) inside a given location range (θrange). Since the
same audio window is used for all quality estimations (just “steered” towards different
locations), a clearer view is provided of the search space at each optimization iteration.
From here on forward, for simplicity, estimating the quality at a given location is
referred to as “testing a location”.
From preliminary evaluations, it was observed that using the output of the whole
speech enhancement module (the output of the Demucs-based speech enhancer)
resulted in a flat search space, while requiring a considerable amount of computing
resources. Thus, it was decided to use only the output of the beamformer stage of the
speech enhancement module, which resulted in a more convex search space as well
requiring less computing resources. Furthermore, in addition to the phase-based fre-
quency masking (PHASE) beamformer [16] used in the original implementation of the
speech enhancer, the minimum variance distortion-less response (MVDR) beamformer
[17–19] was also evaluated since it is less prone to output phase-induced artifacts with
real-life reverberant acoustic scenarios (further details on the evaluation methodology
are provided in Section 4).
Once the set of quality estimations (Qset) are calculated for all the locations (θset)
in the given θrange, these are unified into one quality estimation (θc) via a simple
weighted average, with the quality estimation values (Qset) acting as weights for their
corresponding locations (θset). Algorithm 3 presents how the set of quality estima-
tions are calculated, while the new proposed optimization mechanism is presented in

As it can be seen, the optimization process is considerably simplified. However, it
is important to mention that the quality estimation response time is extended by the
amount of tested locations. Fortunately, as reported in [11], with moderate hardware,
Squim’s response time is between 0.0538 s. and 0.0704 s. for tw = 3.0, which means
that there is enough time to test several locations for a given tw window while still
remaining below the real-time threshold.

The reasoning behind this new optimization mechanism is that carrying out a
weighted average, as presented in Algorithm 4, can be considered as a simplified inter-
polation of the quality estimated in a range of locations (θrange). It should provide a
clearer view of the current search space compared to only having the quality estimated
from one location. By forcing the weighted average to be inside the location range,
the next optimization iteration will estimate the quality of a location range overlap-
ping with the current one, which reduces overshooting. This reasoning is summarized
in Figure 3. The solid blue line in the right is the complete location-vs-quality search
space, which is unknown to the optimization process. Each blue line to its left repre-
sents one optimization iteration, along with its corresponding search space range (also
unknown) which is limited by its respective location range. The red dots with black
border are the θmid of their respective optimization iteration; all red dots represent a
tested location; green dots are the weighted average of their respective iteration. The
green area is where the correct location resides. As it can be seen, by testing a set
of locations at each optimization iteration (instead of just one), and carrying out a
simple weighted average, the whole optimization process reaches the correct location.

It is important to note that the quality estimation agent is only providing a set of
quality estimations and that the optimization agent is only carrying out a weighted
average of these estimations. The optimization process is not being carried out in a
single agent, as was the case in the previous optimization mechanisms. Meaning, the
optimization process is actually a behavior that emerges from the interaction between
these two agents. Thus, not only is the optimization process greatly simplified, but
thanks to these agents’ interaction, a robust optimization process emerges in a global
manner. This is even more evidence of the benefits of modeling the auditory scene
analysis as a multi-agent system: robust results with less overall complexity.
Furthermore, the proposed optimization mechanism can be ‘calibrated’ with both
the θrange and θsteps parameters, which act in conjunction as a type of ‘update fac-
tor’ for the emerging optimization mechanism. However, it is essential to characterize
the behavior of the proposed optimization mechanism with different values of the
[θrange, θsteps] pair, referred here on forward as an ‘optimization configuration’.

4 Evaluation and Results
In this section, several evaluations are carried out to characterize both the parame-
ters of the proposed optimization mechanism: the tested location range (θrange) and
the amount of tested locations inside such range (θsteps). This characterization has
the objective to establish a range of values for each parameter that provide a small
optimization time as well as a localization correction that is robust against varying
acoustic scenarios and number of interferences. Additionally, a comparison is provided
between the proposed optimization mechanism (using the recommended values of the
aforementioned parameters) and the previous optimization mechanism presented in
[12].
4.1 Material
The Acoustic Interactions for Robot Audition (AIRA) corpus [20] is conformed by a
considerable amount of recordings, with varying number of speech sources, in differ-
ent real-life acoustic settings, with different microphone array geometries. In both [11]
and [12], the “Anechoic Chamber” scenario was used to evaluate their respective opti-
mization mechanisms, using a 3-microphone array in the presence of 2 speech sources.
In [12], additionally, the “Office A” scenario, as well as the “Department Store” sce-
nario, (both using a similar 3-microphone array) were also employed to evaluate the
generality of its optimization mechanism with varying amounts of speech sources.
The “Department Store” scenario is comparable to a Walmart or Tesco, and has
a high overall noise level (63 dB SPL) and a moderate level of reverberation (τ60 =
0.16s). The “Office A” scenario is comparable to a computer laboratory, and has a
lower overall noise level (52 dB SPL) and a higher level of reverberation (τ60 = 0.20s).
However, in both [11] and [12], it was only with the “Anechoic Chamber” scenario
that parameters were characterized, which has virtually no noise nor reverberation.
Additionally, when employing the “Office A” and “Department Store”, only a small
amount of localization error was explored to be corrected (10◦).
In this work, the “Office A” scenario, using a 3-microphone array in the presence of
2 speech sources, is employed to characterize the parameters in a real-life environment.
Afterwards, the proposed optimization mechanism is evaluated with varying amounts
of speech sources in that scenario, as well as in the “Department Store” scenario of
the AIRA corpus.
The 3-microphone array geometry is an equilateral triangle with an inter-
microphone distance of 0.18 m. The 2 speech sources are located in 0◦and 90◦
respectively, 1 m. away from the center of the microphone array. Unless stated
otherwise, the speech source of interest is the one located at 0◦.
4.2 Methodology
To carry out repeatable evaluations, an agent feeds the multi-channel recording in
real-time to the rest of the agents to emulate a live microphone using the JACK Audio
Connection Kit [21]. This emulation agent reads 1024-sample windows at 48 kHz
(equating to around 0.021 s. latency), as it was captured for the AIRA corpus. The
emulation agent also provides a 16 kHz stream of the same multi-channel recording,
10


as it is required by Squim. Finally, to provide sufficient evaluation time, the recording
(which is originally 30 s. long) is repeated 4 times, resulting in an evaluation time of
approximately 120 s. Additionally, an artificial localization error (θerror) is applied at
the beginning of the run, which the proposed optimization mechanism is expected to
correct.
The quality estimation agent receives the multi-channel audio data directly from
the emulation agent, storing the last tw = 3.0 s. of audio information and awaiting
requests from the optimization agent. In turn, the optimization agent waits th = 0.25
s. to carry out a request. It is important to mention that th could be lowered to reduce
the overall response time, but it was set at this value to reduce computational load.
The computer used to carry out all of the evaluations uses an AMD Ryzen 5 3600 (6
cores with a 3.6 GHz base clock) and a GTX 1050 Ti with 4 GB of VRAM. This setup
was chosen because it is representative of a computer with moderate computational
capabilities that is within range of the current average consumer.
4.3 Variables and Metrics
Two beamforming techniques are evaluated: the phase-based frequency masking beam-
former (PHASE) [16] used in the original implementation of the speech enhancer, and
the popular minimum variance distortion-less response beamformer (MVDR) [17–19].
Additionally, Squim not only provides an estimation of the signal-to-distortion
ration (SDR), but also of the short-time objective intelligibility (STOI) measure [22]
as well as of the perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ) measure [23]. All
three evaluation measures are evaluated.
Finally, as for optimization configurations, defined by the θrange and θsteps
parameters, a range of values for each are evaluated. For θrange, the values are
[5.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, 25.0]; these values represent the tested location range (in degrees)
that the quality estimation agent is going to use. For θsteps, the values are [3, 5, 7, 9, 11];
these values represent the amount of locations that are going to be tested by the quality
estimation agent along θrange, including the requested ‘middle’ location (θmid).
To this effect, there are a total of four evaluation variables: beamform type, quality
type, θrange, and θsteps. For each combination of values of these evaluation variables,
10 ‘runs’ are carried out running the real-time emulated 120 s. recording, while the
series of corrected locations provided by the optimization agent (θcorr) is recorded for
each run.
Unless stated otherwise, each of the runs employed θerror = 15.0◦, which is
higher than when evaluating the optimization mechanism presented in [12] in different
acoustic settings.
Several metrics are used to characterize and evaluate the proposed optimization
mechanism:
• Accuracy (RMS): the root mean square localization error is used to measure how
accurate is the optimization process. Only the last 3/4 of the run is employed for
calculating this metric so as to measure the accuracy where the optimization process
should have stabilized. Additionally, by calculating this metric in this manner, it is
also useful for discarding slow converging configurations, since these will have large
11


errors at the start of the run. If the metric would had been calculated with the
whole run, it would had suffered from a large bias towards θerror in these cases.
• Variance (σRMS): the standard deviation of the localization error of the last 3/4
of the run is used to measure the magnitude of the oscilations of the optimization
process. It is representative of the stability of the system.
• Optimization time (topt): the time it takes to reach the [−5◦, 5◦] range around the
correct location of the target speech source is used to measure the speed of the
optimization process. For simplicity, this range is referred to as the [θcorrect] range
from here on forward.
• Quality estimation latency (tlat): the response time of the quality estimation agent
is used to assess the overall quality estimation latency. It is measured from the
moment the optimization agent makes a request to the quality estimation agent, to
the moment the optimization agent receives a response from the quality estimation
agent.
Additionally, a ‘good run’ is defined as one that has a RMS below 5◦, while the
‘good run’ percentage is measured as the amount of ‘good runs’ divided the amount
of runs for each evaluation variable combination. The ‘good run’ percentage metric is
used to discard inconsistent optimization configurations that perform well only some
of the time.

These results show that there are conflicting tendencies between accuracy and

speed is of interest. To this effect, a set of restrictions were applied to obtain a set

θerror, or number of sources present. This implies that the optimization process is
expected to ‘behave’ similarly in different acoustic scenarios.
5 Conclusion
A real-time auditory scene analyzer (ASA) carries out in conjunction the tasks of
locating, separating, and classifying sound sources in the environment, with important
benefits to application areas such as bioacoustics, hearing aid research, search and
rescue scenarios, human-robot interaction, etc. Recently, there have been efforts to
model an ASA as a multi-agent system, where agents (each in charge of a particular
task) interact with one another to provide local error robustness at the global level,
while reducing overall complexity. In these efforts, the feedback loop between the
localization and separation agents was shown to be able to correct the localization
error by optimizing the separation quality using the reference-free quality estimation
model called Squim. However, it was also shown to be very slow to reach the correct
location, while being relatively complex.
In this work, a new optimization mechanism is proposed that exploits the inter-
action between the quality estimation agent and the optimization agent (which are
part of the aforementioned localization-separation feedback loop). The quality estima-
tion agent, instead of carrying out one quality estimation steered a given location at
each optimization iteration, it estimates several quality estimations, each steered at
a different location (spread from a given middle location). The optimization agent is
reduced to a weighted average, using the quality estimated values as weights, carrying
out a type of simplified interpolation inside a given location range. Although there is
not an optimization process instructed anywhere in the two agents, an optimization
17


behavior emerges from their interaction, with the possibility of being ‘calibrated’ by
ways of modifying the size of the tested location range (θrange) and how many quality
estimations are carried out inside it (θsteps).
The evaluation showed that the proposed optimization mechanism provided good
results in different real-life acoustic scenarios, with varying noise and reverberation
levels, and with varying amounts of sound sources. More importantly, it showed to
be more robust to larger localization errors, provides stabler results, and be faster
to reach the correct location than the previous optimization mechanisms, by a wide
margin in all accounts, while being considerably less complex.
It is important to mention that, given that multiple quality estimations (θsteps)
are being carried out, in the proposed optimization mechanism the quality estimation
agent has a considerably higher response time than the previous optimization mech-
anisms. However, it is still able to satisfy the requirement for real-time operation.
Additionally, given the parallel nature of multi-agent systems, the separation latency
(the time it takes to process a given audio window) is not affected. The quality esti-
mation agent response time does affect the optimization time (the time the whole
system takes to reach the correct location), but an in-depth characterization of the
[θrange, θsteps] optimization parameters resulted in a set of values that provided a good
balance between optimization time, quality estimation response time, and localization
accuracy and stability: [θrange = 20.0, θsteps = 5].
There are two factors that impact the quality estimation response time which, in
turn, impacts the optimization time. The first factor is the amount of quality esti-
mations carried out at every optimization iteration (θsteps), which has already been
characterized in this work. The second factor is the response time of the reference-free
quality estimation model, Squim. It is important to reiterate that, although it is able
to run in real-time, such a model does not consider causality internally; it assumes that
it is being fed a complete recording. Thus, as it was shown in [12], it requires a consid-
erable amount of data to provide even moderately stable results, with 3.0 s. being a
good balance point between stability and latency. For future work, other reference-free
quality estimation models will be explored to find one that provides smaller laten-
cies and stabler results. Another alternative is to modify the Squim model to consider
causality which may reduce its latency, while keeping its current level of stability.
